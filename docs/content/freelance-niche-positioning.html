"I do websites for everyone" is a positioning statement that guarantees you are compared to the cheapest option available. It is not said because it is true — it is said because choosing a specific buyer feels like turning work away. This article explains why the opposite is the case: how narrowing who you serve changes what you can charge, how to pick a niche you can actually win, and how to make the switch without breaking the income you already have.

The direct answer: narrowing works because buyers can only choose you when they recognize themselves in your promise. A specific audience plus a specific outcome makes your pitch relevant to the person reading it, your proof accumulates in one place instead of scattering, and referrals travel as a category people can repeat — "she fixes checkouts for stores like ours" — instead of a skill list they forget. Choosing less is not about doing less work. It is about making the same work legible, and letting it compound.

Why "everyone" puts you in a price comparison

A promise that fits every buyer tells each buyer nothing. When a prospect cannot tell whether you have ever solved their problem, they fall back on the only axis left after similarity fails: cost. And on that axis you are competing not just with other freelancers, but with template builders, in-house time, and agencies bidding a junior at your rate. Being "full-stack" is not a differentiator; in 2026 it is table stakes dressed as a benefit.

  • Generic promises are evaluated on price. No specific problem is named, so no specific value can be either.
  • Generic promises are not referable. People recommend categories, not skill lists — nobody says "you should hire my developer, he knows MySQL."
  • Generic promises hide your best evidence. Three unrelated projects prove you can build things; they do not prove you understand this buyer's Monday morning.
  • A wedge does the filtering for you. "Checkout optimization for DTC brands" turns away almost everyone — which is exactly what makes the remaining reader lean in.
  • Specificity is evaluable in seconds. A stranger can immediately say whether your promise is for them; with "websites for everyone," the honest answer is a shrug.

The narrowing is not a trick to charge more for the same work. It is a claim about relevance — and relevance is the thing buyers are actually shopping for, even when they open the conversation talking about budget.

Anatomy of a positioning statement

Write the positioning before you write the homepage. A working statement has five slots, and each one does a specific job:

For [specific audience] who [trigger or costly problem],
[your name or studio] is a [category of service] that
[outcome they want], unlike [the alternative they would
otherwise pick], you [one differentiator they can verify].
  • Specific audience: nameable and findable — a role plus a context, such as "operations leads at 10–50-person logistics companies," not a demographic like "small businesses."
  • Trigger: the event that makes them buy now. Triggers beat traits: a platform migration, a cart-completion problem, a security audit deadline.
  • Category: sets expectations for process and price ("conversion-focused build partner," not "full-stack developer").
  • Outcome: in their language, never your tools. "Stops losing orders at checkout" beats "implements a headless storefront."
  • Alternative: the real competition — the in-house fix, the agency, the template, or doing nothing. Naming it shows you know the decision they are actually making.
  • Differentiator: exactly one, and it must be verifiable ("I have shipped on your platform" or "I work in two-week increments"), not a self-assessment like "high-quality code."

Treat the statement as a working tool, not a slogan. If it does not feel slightly too narrow, it is too broad. A useful test: say it out loud to a stranger and ask who it is not for. If the answer does not come back quickly, the statement is not doing any positioning yet.

Evaluation criteria: a niche you can actually win

Interests and intuition are weak niche selectors. Score each candidate niche against these criteria before committing — and remember the table's real job is elimination, not discovery:

Scoring rules that keep you honest: rate each criterion from 1 to 5, and treat a 1 on access or problem cost as a veto — those two cannot be compensated with enthusiasm. Two 3s in a row usually means the niche is a project, not a practice. And run the criteria before you fall in love with the idea; affection after evidence is fine, affection instead of evidence is how generalists stay generalists with a fancier label.

Two positioning approaches, side by side (worked example)

Hypothetical freelancer, constructed for illustration — not a real client story: "Sam," a PHP developer with four years of agency experience, a handful of e-commerce builds, some analytics setup, and no strong specialization. Here is the same person positioned two ways.

Approach A — the skill list

"Full-stack developer building websites and web apps for small businesses. PHP, JavaScript, MySQL, WordPress and Laravel. Ten projects delivered."

  • What the buyer hears: nothing about their specific problem, so nothing creates urgency; the evaluation defaults to price, timeline, and trust.
  • Work it attracts: anything and everything — every proposal is written from scratch against a broad field of competitors.
  • Failure mode: a buyer with a real, named problem cannot see themselves in the promise, so they move on without replying.

Approach B — the wedge

"Checkout and cart-flow work for DTC brands: shipping logic that confuses buyers, variant pickers that lose orders, and slow pages at the moment of payment. If cart completion is the problem, that is the whole job I do."

  • What the buyer hears: their own Monday morning, described by a stranger. The question changes from "are you any good?" to "have you seen our situation?"
  • Work it attracts: fewer inquiries, far more that fit; projects begin from a diagnosed problem instead of a blank brief.
  • Proof path: every checkout project makes the next checkout conversation shorter, because the evidence stacks in one place.
  • Honest risk: narrower means thinner at first — if the niche turns out to lack budget or access, the wedge starves. That is what the criteria table and the tests below exist to catch.

Neither approach guarantees work. The wedge makes you legible, which is a precondition for being chosen — not a promise of it. And the example is deliberately hypothetical: the mechanism (specificity → relevance → compounding proof) is what transfers to your situation, not Sam's particular choice.

Testing the niche before you commit

You are not signing a life contract; you are choosing a direction with evidence. Narrowing again later is cheap — broadening back out later is not. Run these tests in order, and stay on each until it produces a real answer:

  1. Pattern scan: read your last ten projects and circle every recurring problem — not the tools you used. The circled problems are your candidate wedges, and they are chosen by evidence rather than ambition.
  2. Access test: list fifteen organizations or people in the candidate niche. If you cannot reach fifteen, or cannot say where they gather, this niche fails on access no matter how interesting it sounds.
  3. Language scan: read job posts, RFPs, and community threads from the niche and copy the exact phrases they use for the pain. Your positioning must use their words, not yours.
  4. Payment sanity check: look for visible signs the problem is paid for — other specialists serving it, budget in proposals, recurring need. No spend signals, no niche.
  5. Say-it-out-loud test: state the positioning to two people in the niche and ask who it is obviously not for. A good statement gets a fast, specific answer; a bad one gets politeness.
  6. Time box: commit for a defined run — a quarter is a common choice — and judge it on fit and the quality of inbound interest, not on a single month's pipeline.

If a niche fails the access test or the payment sanity check, do not negotiate with the result. Kill it and score the next candidate; that rejection costs an afternoon, while a year in the wrong niche costs the compounding you were trying to build.

Proof compounds

Three case studies in one niche make the fourth conversation shorter; general work never accumulates that way. The mechanism is pattern-matching: after three checkout projects, the next buyer can see that you have met their problem before, and your case study answers their questions before they ask them. With three unrelated projects, every conversation starts from zero.

  • Repeated vocabulary compounds. The fourth pitch reuses the industry's words, failure modes, and integration partners instead of learning them on the client's clock.
  • Each piece of proof removes one objection. One case study for "shipping logic," one for "variant pickers," one for "slow pages" — and the fourth buyer's three worries are already answered.
  • Specialists get referred inside the niche. A DTC founder's friends are DTC founders; a generalist's proof does not survive that referral path.
  • One niche case study beats five general portfolio pieces. A niche case study is a magnet; a general portfolio is a museum — interesting, and never the reason someone hires.

Structure each case study the same way: the problem in their language; the constraints that match theirs (platform, team size, traffic class); what you actually did; what changed, with evidence you are permitted to publish; and one clear next step for the reader. Permission edge case: much client work is confidential, and publishing numbers without approval is how you lose the client. Publish the method instead — describe the problem class, your diagnosis steps, and the shape of the fix — and keep the identifying details out. For the page that holds all this together, a portfolio built so a stranger can answer "can you ship?" in seconds covers the structure.

Raising rates is a repositioning moment

A price rise is a claim about value. Announced under the old promise, the client hears only cost — "same work, more money" — and every existing relationship absorbs it as a slight. New positioning first, new prices second. Change what you claim, and the number becomes a consequence of the claim rather than an unexplained increase.

  1. Rewrite the statement and put it everywhere a stranger meets you: bio, homepage, proposal template, every profile you forgot existed.
  2. Publish one piece of niche proof — a case study or a detailed method write-up — so the new promise arrives with evidence attached.
  3. Requote new prospects immediately at the new rate. Strangers need no transition period; they never knew the old number.
  4. Give existing clients notice and a transition, in writing, before the next quote. Something like: "From [date], new projects are quoted at my updated rate, as my work is now focused on [niche outcome]. Your current project stays at the price we agreed, and I'll send updated terms with plenty of notice for anything after that."
  • Decide your grandfathering policy deliberately — a fixed window (say, one quarter at old rates), a capped discount, or none at all. Any is defensible if it is stated; silently discounting forever is how long-term clients become your least profitable ones.
  • Do the arithmetic against the rate-floor method in the pricing and burnout guide before you pick the new number — repositioning changes what you can quote, not what you need to earn.
  • If demand is thin after the switch, the answer is usually more proof or a narrower wedge — not a lower price announced halfway through a repositioning.

Common mistakes and honest limitations

  • Choosing by enthusiasm or a listicle instead of demand, access, and proof transfer — excitement is not evidence.
  • Mistaking a tech stack for a niche. "Laravel developer" is still a skill list; the niche names a buyer's problem, not your tools.
  • Too narrow too early — a wedge you cannot feed yourself with for a year starves the proof loop before it starts.
  • Too broad to distinguish: "SaaS companies" is thousands of buyers and zero specificity.
  • Niche-hopping every quarter. Proof never accumulates, which defeats the entire mechanism you were switching for.
  • Positioning only on the website while proposals still say "open to any PHP work" — the promise has to appear in every artifact a buyer touches.
  • Telling long-term clients you "don't do their kind of work" abruptly. Transition them; do not break them.
  • Honest limitations: none of this guarantees clients, referrals, or a particular rate — markets vary, and the wedge fails without access and budget. The scenarios above are labeled hypothetical illustrations, not case studies, and the first months of a niche usually feel quieter than the generalist work they replace.

Key takeaways

  • "For everyone" is a positioning statement about price; a specific audience plus a specific outcome is a positioning statement about relevance
  • A five-slot statement — audience, trigger, category, outcome, alternative — is a working tool; if it feels comfortable, it is too broad
  • Evaluate niches on cost/frequency of the problem, access, proof transfer, competition, pricing power, delivery fit, and durability; veto on access or cost
  • Test cheaply before committing: pattern scan, access list, language scan, payment sanity check, say-it-out-loud, then a time box
  • Proof compounds inside one niche and resets outside it — one niche case study beats five general portfolio pieces
  • Price rises are repositioning moments: new promise first, new numbers second, existing clients transitioned in writing
  • Narrowing is a direction chosen with evidence, not a lifetime vow — re-evaluate at the end of the time box
