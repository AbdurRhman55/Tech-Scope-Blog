Focus is not a personality trait — it is an environment outcome. The people who produce deep work are not wired differently; they have arranged their day, their tools and their commitments so that concentration is the path of least resistance and interruption is expensive. Stop trying to become disciplined and start designing defaults.

This article is a working system, not a philosophy: how to pick a realistic number of focus blocks, what a defended day actually looks like hour by hour, how to design the environment so you do not have to resist anything, a protocol for the interruptions that will come anyway, a shutdown ritual that ends rumination, and — the part most advice skips — how to repair the system on the days it inevitably breaks.

Why willpower fails by lunchtime

Willpower is a finite, unreliable resource that degrades with every decision you make between morning and afternoon. Any plan that assumes you will "just stay focused" for eight hours is really assuming you will win a hundred small arguments with your phone, your inbox and your colleagues — and lose none. Nobody wins all of them.

The system approach replaces resisting with arranging. Three design principles run through everything below:

  • Decide once, not continuously. The block, the task and the rules are chosen in advance; during the block there is nothing left to decide.
  • Raise the cost of distraction. Anything distracting should be at least thirty seconds of effort away; anything productive should be one click away.
  • Lower the cost of starting. The next task is written down, openable and unambiguous, because the expensive moment is the first one.

Deep work here means any stretch where you are producing — writing, designing, coding, analysing — rather than consuming or coordinating. Shallow work is real and necessary; the goal is not to abolish it but to stop letting it eat the hours where your best thinking is available.

Two blocks, not eight hours

Two defended 90-minute blocks with a single task each beat eight hours of vague availability, for a simple accounting reason: vague availability is infinitely divisible, and everything — meetings, chat, errands, other people's priorities — divides it. A block that already has a name and a task cannot be quietly repurposed.

Why ninety minutes as the default, with sixty as the fallback:

  • It is long enough to cross the expensive part of a task — the setup, the context reconstruction, the first wrong attempt — and reach actual production.
  • It is short enough that you can defend two of them on an ordinary day without your calendar revolting.
  • It has a hard edge, which does two jobs: it starts by appointment (no "when I feel ready"), and it ends before fatigue turns into a three-hour foggy session.

Put the blocks in the calendar as meetings with yourself — recurring, titled after the task, with alerts off. The calendar entry is the defence: a colleague scheduling over "Focus block" sees that you are busy; a colleague scheduling over "free" sees opportunity. Two blocks a day, most weekdays, is a realistic sustainable load for most knowledge workers — the professionals who try five blocks a day tend to abandon the whole system in week two.

Environment design: make distraction expensive

Every rule below is a way of not needing willpower at minute forty of a block. They are ordered roughly by leverage:

  • Phone in another room. Distance beats self-control — the point is not to resist the notification, it is to make picking the phone up cost a walk. This single change is usually the largest improvement available.
  • Website blockers during blocks. It is not cheating; it is scaffolding. Block the short list of sites that absorb twenty minutes at a time, and schedule the unblocking for outside the block so you cannot bargain with yourself.
  • Close chat apps; notifications are other people's priorities arriving on your screen. Set a status ("focus until 10:15") so silence is announced rather than interpreted. Most things that feel urgent are answered in the next shallow window.
  • One task per block, written on paper beside the keyboard. Tab-switching to "check what I was doing" is where blocks go to die.
  • Prepare the starting position the day before. Last tab open: the file, the document, the query you were mid-way through. Morning-you should be one double-click from production.
  • Control the physical scene. Headphones as a social signal, a door that closes if you have one, water on the desk — remove the small reasons to stand up, because each stand-up is an ambush opportunity for something else.
  • Keep a scratchpad in the corner of the desk. For "email the plumber", "look up that library later" — parked items get written, not dwelled on, and collected in the shallow window.

The interruptions protocol

Interruptions will come anyway — a colleague, a Slack ping that escaped the filter, your own brain with a brilliant unrelated thought. The system needs a written response, not a heroic one:

  • Colleague interrupts you. "I'm in a focus block until 10:15 — can I come to you then?" Most of the time the answer is yes, and occasionally it is a genuine fire — which you handle by ending the block, not by splitting it.
  • A message arrives. Do not reply during the block; reply in the shallow window. Replying "quickly" to one message teaches everyone, including you, that your blocks are soft.
  • A task pops into your head. Write it on the scratchpad, in one line, and return to the block. The parking lot is what lets you stop thinking about it.
  • Truly urgent work arrives. End the block cleanly: note where you stopped in the scratchpad ("left off: index on bookings table, EXPLAIN next"), handle the urgent item, and if possible take the block's second half later that day.
  • You notice you have drifted. Do not audit yourself mid-block; write the drifting thought down and steer back. Guilt is also a context switch.

Teams can adopt this too: an agreed focus signal (status colour, headset convention), a definition of what counts as interrupt-worthy, and an expectation that anything non-urgent waits for the next shallow window. A written team norm beats individual heroics every time.

End with a shutdown

The last fifteen minutes determine how the next morning starts. A shutdown ritual does two jobs: it catches loose ends before they float around your head all evening, and it gives tomorrow-you a starting task instead of an inbox. Keep it short and fixed:

  • Process the scratchpad: delete, schedule, or write each parked item into its real destination.
  • Check tomorrow's calendar once, and move any deep block that a new meeting has buried.
  • Write tomorrow's single priority — one task, the one that makes the day count.
  • Close every tab and application that does not belong to tomorrow's first step.
  • Say the shutdown out loud if it helps: "shutdown complete." The verbal edge sounds silly and works — your brain lets go when it trusts the plan.

The stance is best captured in one line worth pinning above the desk:

You do not find time for deep work. You defend it, loudly.

When the system breaks: troubleshooting

It will break — travel, a sick week, a crunch, a product launch. Systems that cannot survive contact with a bad fortnight are hobbies. Here is the repair table:

SymptomLikely causeFix
Blocks kept getting booked overYour calendar says the time is free; nobody can see your intentRecreate the blocks as visible events with a work-sounding title; renegotiate one standing meeting rather than five ad-hoc ones
You sit down and can't startNo defined first step; the task is too large to enterBreak the block's task into a first action that takes under five minutes ("open the migration file, re-read the schema")
You drift to the phone within ten minutesWithdrawal from an unbroken streak; the block is too ambitiousHalve the block length for a week; phone still in another room; rebuild the streak before rebuilding the duration
Deep work happens, but only after 4 p.m.Your chronotype or meeting pattern puts real focus laterMove block 2 to your peak hour and treat the morning as shallow — the system bends to reality
Shutdown never happens; evenings bleed into workNo closing edge, or guilt about unfinished itemsWrite the unfinished list explicitly — named items stop being worries; keep the ritual at ten minutes maximum
After two weeks it all collapsesYou designed for an ideal day that does not existRedesign for your worst recurring day first (meetings-heavy Tuesdays), then scale up on the good ones

Honest limits

This system has boundaries, and pretending otherwise is how productivity advice becomes self-help theatre:

  • Some jobs cannot guarantee two blocks. Open-plan offices, on-call rotations, care duties and heavily meeting-bound roles all compress the available surface. Two blocks four days a week, or one block six days, still beats a nonexistent five-day ideal.
  • Deep work is not the only valuable work. Answering colleagues quickly, mentoring juniors and unblocking others are real contributions; a system that optimises your output while degrading your team is a net loss, and usually a career loss too.
  • Not every brain suits the same shape. People with ADHD or anxiety frequently need external structure — body doubling, shorter blocks, more visible accountability — rather than more resolve. The principles (decide once, raise distraction cost, lower starting cost) still apply; the implementation changes.
  • Focus without recovery becomes burnout. The shutdown ritual and the real lunch break are load-bearing. If you are defending focus while never defending rest, the schedule is extracting from you — the sustainability angle in our piece on careers that last applies to employed life just as much.
  • Deep work is not a substitute for direction. Two focused hours on the wrong task are two focused hours lost; five minutes at the start of the day choosing the task is not overhead.

Key takeaways

  • Focus is an environment outcome: decide once, raise the cost of distraction, lower the cost of starting.
  • Two defended 90-minute blocks with one named task each beat eight hours of vague availability.
  • Put blocks in the calendar as visible events; schedule shallow and collaborative work explicitly so it stops eating everything.
  • Phone in another room, blockers on, chat closed, one task on paper, starting position prepared the night before.
  • Run an interruption protocol in writing — defer messages, park stray thoughts, end dirty blocks with a "left off" note.
  • Shutdown daily: process the scratchpad and write tomorrow's single priority, so the evening is actually over.
  • Repair the system with the troubleshooting table instead of abandoning it after a bad week.

Your next step takes fifteen minutes: put tomorrow's first 90-minute block in the calendar right now, with the task named in the title, and write the block's first five-minute action on a sticky note. Then do the one thing the system depends on — tell one person you are unavailable during it. The notes you generate in these blocks will want a home when the block ends; a lightweight linked-note practice like Zettelkasten is how the thinking outlives the session, and the same retrieval-first logic makes it easy to learn hard material in defended blocks like these.
