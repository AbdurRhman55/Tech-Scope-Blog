Nobody reads a paper front to back. Researchers skim strategically, and so should you. Reading a paper is not an act of completion — it is an act of triage: your goal is to extract the maximum reliable understanding per minute, and to know, quickly, which papers deserve a second hour and which deserve none.

The direct answer: use a three-pass method. Pass 1 is a five-minute scan to decide if the paper is relevant at all. Pass 2 is roughly an hour of reading without proofs, focused on claims, methods and whether the evidence supports the claims. Pass 3 is a deep dive — re-deriving results and checking assumptions — reserved only for papers you will build on. Each pass has a fixed goal and a fixed output, so you always know when you are done.

The three passes at a glance

PassTime budgetGoalOutput you should hold
Pass 1 — scanAbout five minutesDecide relevance and orientationThree-to-five-sentence summary; keep, skim later, or discard
Pass 2 — critical readAbout an hourUnderstand the claims and judge the evidenceClaim list, method notes, a verdict on each claim
Pass 3 — deep diveSeveral hours, only if justifiedVerify and internalise what you will depend onRe-derived results, checked assumptions, your own critique

The time budgets are conventions, not laws — but they are useful because they force a decision. If a paper has not earned your second pass within five minutes, it goes back to the pile with a note about why.

Before pass 1: decide whether to read at all

Most papers you encounter should not be read at all. They should be triaged. Before you open the file, ask:

  • Why did this paper reach me? A citation from a colleague, a reference in a paper you already trust, a search for a specific technique — each implies a different reading depth.
  • What question am I trying to answer? If you cannot state it in one sentence, you are collecting PDFs, not reading papers.
  • Is this the right venue and vintage for a first look? A paper's context — where it was published, when, and what problem class it belongs to — changes how much trust to place in its framing.
  • Can I get the answer faster elsewhere? Sometimes a specification, documentation page or textbook section answers your actual question; papers answer research questions.

Write down the purpose before you start. A reading session without a stated purpose expands to fill all available time and produces nothing you can find again in three weeks.

Pass 1 — the five-minute scan

The original instruction still holds: title, abstract, section headings, figures, conclusion. What changes now is that you read them with a pencil, extracting specific items rather than impressions.

What to extract in pass 1:

  • Category and problem. What kind of contribution is this — a new method, a measurement study, a position argument, a system description?
  • The central claim, in your own words, one sentence. If the abstract does not yield one, that itself is a finding.
  • The structure map. Skim the section headings and ask what each section is doing. A paper you can outline from its headings is a paper you can navigate.
  • Figures and tables first. In many fields, the visuals carry the argument. Read captions; a good figure often tells you the result without the prose.
  • The verdict paragraph. Does the conclusion claim more than the abstract? A gap between the two is worth noting early.
  • Reference density. Do you recognise the lineage it cites? That tells you what background you would need for pass 2.

Decision at the end of pass 1: discard with a one-line reason, park for later, or commit to pass 2. The one-line reason matters — "parked: methods not relevant, but the related-work list is useful" prevents you from re-reading it from scratch next month.

Pass 2 — the critical hour

In pass 2 you read without proofs and derivations, following the argument rather than checking it. The goal is to answer two questions: what exactly is claimed? and does the evidence presented actually support it?

What to extract in pass 2:

  • The full claim list. Number every distinct claim the paper makes. Authors often compress several claims into one sentence; separating them is most of the analytical work.
  • The method skeleton. What was done, to what, measured how, compared against what baseline? Sketch it as five bullet points, not paragraphs.
  • Assumptions — stated and unstated. What must be true for the results to hold: data distribution, hardware, workload, population, time period?
  • Evidence-to-claim mapping. For each claim, mark which figure, table or experiment supports it. Claims with no mapped evidence are now flagged.
  • Limitations the authors admit — and one or two they do not. Reading limitations section first is a legitimate shortcut: it is usually the most honest part of a paper.
  • Your questions. Every "why didn't they…?" and "does this generalise to…?" goes into the margin. These become pass 3 candidates or research ideas.

Skip the proofs on purpose. If a key derivation matters and you do not follow it, note that as a pass 3 item rather than derailing the whole hour. Finishing pass 2 with a written paragraph of judgement — "the evidence supports claim 1, weakly supports claim 2, and does not address claim 3" — is worth more than having read every line.

Reading everything deeply is not diligence — it is a recipe for never finishing.

Pass 3 — the deep dive

Pass 3 is for papers you will build on: the one your own work depends on, the method you plan to implement, the result you intend to cite in a paper of your own. Most papers should never reach this stage.

What to extract in pass 3:

  • Re-derived results. Work through the key derivation or calculation yourself on paper. Where your algebra and theirs diverge is where understanding lives.
  • Checked assumptions. Interrogate each assumption against your own context. "Valid for images of this size" is a fact you need before you reuse the method.
  • A partial reproduction. If the paper ships code or pseudocode, implement the smallest piece that would falsify the central claim if it failed. This is where our companion guide on building a reproducible research environment becomes practical: an environment you cannot rerun is an environment where "let's check their result" dies at the first dependency error.
  • Boundary cases. Where should the method break? Authors rarely stress-test their own framing; doing it for them tells you whether it will hold in your setting.
  • What you would do differently. One paragraph of alternative design. This is the seed of future work, and the surest sign you have actually understood the paper.
  • Citation-quality judgement. Decide, explicitly, how you are allowed to use this paper — as established fact, as one datapoint, or as a claim you have verified yourself.

Time-box pass 3 ruthlessly. A week in, if you are re-reading the introduction instead of re-deriving results, you are procrastinating inside a PDF.

A worked example (invented for this article)

To make the mechanics concrete, here is the method applied to a hypothetical paper invented purely for illustration: a fictional systems paper titled "Bandwidth-Aware Caching for Tiered Storage". No such study is being cited; every detail below exists to show what an extraction looks like.

Pass 1 — five minutes, out loud

  • Category: systems method paper with a simulation-based evaluation.
  • Central claim (one sentence): an eviction policy that prices objects by transfer bandwidth keeps hit latency lower than LRU-style policies under mixed hot/cold workloads.
  • Structure: intro → design → simulator → workload description → results → related work. The workload section is where I will need to look hardest.
  • Visuals: three latency curves and one capacity chart, all labelled with the same two baselines. The captions alone show the paper's whole argument shape.
  • Verdict: relevant — proceed to pass 2, park the related-work list for later.

Pass 2 — one hour, no derivations

  • Claim 1: lower p95 latency than LRU on the mixed workload — supported by figure 2.
  • Claim 2: gains persist as cache capacity shrinks — supported by figure 3, but only for one capacity range.
  • Claim 3 (implicit): the policy is practical to deploy — no direct evidence: no implementation beyond the simulator, no cost of the policy's own bookkeeping. Flagged.
  • Method skeleton: discrete-event simulator, two synthetic workloads plus one trace, three policies compared, single run per configuration.
  • Assumptions noted: object sizes drawn from a fixed distribution; no request-correlation modelling; single-node storage.
  • Written verdict: claims 1 and 2 supported within the simulated setting; claim 3 is an extrapolation. Useful, with conditions.

Pass 3 — only because our hypothetical project depends on it

  • Re-derive the eviction score for one toy trace and confirm it orders objects as the design section says.
  • Check whether the workload assumptions match our real access pattern — if they do not, claims 1 and 2 need re-testing locally before we cite them.
  • Reproduce the smallest figure: one policy, one workload, ten-minute run, in a pinned environment so the result is repeatable.
  • Note what we would do differently: report the bookkeeping overhead the paper omits.

Notice what did not happen: no line-by-line reading, no proof-checking in pass 2, no reproduction of the full evaluation. The output is a judgement and an evidence trail, not a finished book.

Notes worth keeping (and how to hook them up)

Reading without writing is reading you will repeat. Keep the notes small, structured and findable:

  • A citation header: authors, title, year, venue, and where you found it — enough to relocate the PDF without searching.
  • The one-sentence claim from pass 1. Always in your own words; copying the abstract's phrasing proves nothing.
  • The claim/evidence table from pass 2 — one row per claim, one column for its support.
  • Open questions from both passes, so a future reading session starts where the last one stopped.
  • Reuse rights: established / one datapoint / verified myself — the judgement you will need a year from now when a reviewer asks where a claim came from.

Where those notes live matters less than that they are linked to something you actually revisit. If your reading feeds a personal knowledge system, file the note where the links will be useful — see note-taking built on connections rather than folders. And if you plan to use the findings as study material rather than reference material, the evidence-based techniques for remembering what you learn — spaced retrieval over re-reading — apply to papers too: a claim you summarised once and never revisited decays like any other memorised fact.

Reading papers you cannot fully follow

You will hit papers where the notation, the subfield or the statistics are beyond you. Honest options, roughly in order:

  • Read around, not through. Pull the two or three references that carry the background and read their abstracts first; the gap often closes in one step.
  • Follow a single thread. You do not need every section to answer one question. Extracting "how do they define the baseline" from a paper you only half understand is a legitimate result.
  • Use the supplementary material. Appendices, glossaries and released code frequently explain more clearly than the prose.
  • Ask. A colleague, a supervisor, the authors themselves — a short, specific question ("in section 4, is X assumed or derived?") gets short answers.
  • Say so. Note "understood claims and method, skipped the proof of theorem 2" rather than pretending to a comprehension you do not have. Faked understanding compounds worse than recorded gaps.

Common mistakes

  • Reading linearly, every time. Front-to-back is a strategy for exactly one situation: a paper you already decided deserves pass 3.
  • Highlighting instead of writing. A page of yellow proves attention, not comprehension. Two sentences of your own words prove comprehension.
  • No stated purpose. "I should read this" is not a question. Questions end; vague obligations do not.
  • Skipping the methods section. Claims without methods are marketing. In pass 2, methods is where you spend the hour.
  • Trusting the abstract as summary. Abstracts are written to survive review; conclusions sometimes admit what abstracts omit.
  • Letting the pile grow unchecked. A to-read list without triage dates is a guilt inventory. Re-run pass 1 on anything older than a few weeks — most of it discards itself.
  • Skipping the verdict. If you finished a pass and did not write what you now believe, the pass did not happen.

Key takeaways

  • Three passes, three purposes: scan to decide, read to judge, dive to verify — and a stopping rule for each.
  • Every pass produces an artifact: a summary, a claim/evidence table, or a re-derived result. Reading without an artifact is skimming with extra steps.
  • Most papers stop after pass 1 or 2. Depth is a budget, not a virtue.
  • Evidence-to-claim mapping is the core critical skill — it turns "this seems solid" into a judgement you can defend.
  • Notes are the return on your investment; unlinked highlights are a highlighter's diary.
  • Premature pass 3 is procrastination in disguise; never reaching pass 3 on work you depend on is how errors propagate.

Your next step: take the paper at the top of your reading pile and run a strictly timed pass 1 — five minutes, one written paragraph, one keep/park/discard decision. Tomorrow, give the keepers an hour of pass 2 with a claim list. Once a paper survives both, that is your signal to schedule a pass 3, with a pinned environment ready for whatever you decide to reproduce.
