Passwords failed because they asked humans to be machines. Passkeys succeed because they ask machines to be machines.

The direct answer: a passkey is a key pair created on the user's device; the private key never leaves it, the public key lives on your server, and login is a cryptographic signature over a fresh challenge instead of a shared secret. Passkeys succeed where SMS codes failed because there is nothing to phish, nothing reusable to steal from your database, and no delivery channel to intercept — and they only work on the domain they were created for. Below: how the ceremony works, what browsers actually support (a dated table you must re-verify before shipping), and a step-by-step rollout for an existing password-based application.

Why passwords and SMS codes failed

Both systems depend on a secret traveling through a channel that can be observed or intercepted. Removing the secret — rather than adding another code on top of it — is the structural fix.

  • Reusability is the vulnerability. A password works on every site that accepts it, so one leaked database fuels account takeovers on unrelated sites that accept the same credential.
  • Human-generated secrets are predictable. Asking people to invent and remember unique strings produces reuse, rotation games and sticky notes — the machine was given a job humans do badly.
  • SMS codes add a channel, not a secret. A one-time code is still a typed value that a fake site can ask for in real time, and the delivery path depends on the telecom rather than on your application.
  • One-time codes are shareable and replayable within their window. Anything a human can read aloud to a support agent can be read aloud to an attacker.
  • Server breaches leak the useful half. Password databases — hashed or not — give an attacker something worth cracking; a database of public keys gives them nothing that signs anything.

The pattern underneath: an authentication method is only as strong as the weakest thing an attacker can obtain. Move from "something the human remembers and types" to "something the device signs with, after the human proves presence locally", and both the phishable artifact and the stealable secret disappear.

How a passkey works

A key pair is generated on your device. The private key never leaves; the public key lives on the server. Login is a cryptographic signature.

Registration

  1. Your application sends a random challenge, bound to the user's session, plus the user identifier and a display name.
  2. The authenticator — a phone, laptop secure element, or a password manager acting as one — generates a new asymmetric key pair scoped to your domain.
  3. The user approves the operation locally with a biometric or device PIN (local user verification).
  4. The public key, credential identifier and metadata are returned and stored server-side. Nothing secret is stored.

Authentication

  1. Your application issues a fresh challenge — never one that has been used before.
  2. The authenticator checks that it is talking to the correct origin, verifies the user locally, and signs the challenge with the private key.
  3. Your server verifies the signature against the stored public key and opens a session.
  4. Server-side session handling then behaves exactly as it does after a password login — cookies, expiry, CSRF, all unchanged.

Two implementation notes that are easy to miss:

  • Scope everything to the origin. The credential is bound to your relying party ID (your domain) at creation, and the browser enforces that binding at sign-in. Subdomains and staging hosts need deliberate configuration, not luck.
  • Never create a passkey for an unauthenticated user. Registration must happen from an authenticated session (or immediately after a verified login), or an attacker can register their passkey on someone else's account.

Why phishing resistance comes for free

Phishing resistance comes for free: a passkey only works on the domain it was created for. No training, no vigilance, no "check the URL" — the binding is enforced by the browser and the authenticator together.

  • Origin binding is automatic. The authenticator will not sign for a look-alike domain, so a perfect replica of your login page receives nothing it can use.
  • No typed secret exists to capture. Keyloggers and overlay attacks have nothing to read; the signature is generated for one challenge only.
  • Local verification gates every signature. Unlocking the device with a biometric or PIN happens before the private key is usable.
  • There is no shared secret on your server. The stored public key cannot be replayed as a login.
  • The caveat is your recovery flow. An attacker who can talk your support team into resetting an account bypasses the cryptography — recovery design is part of the security model, not an afterthought.

Rollout steps for a real app

The advice that keeps launches calm: offer passkeys alongside passwords, never instead of them at first. Then earn the right to remove the password.

  1. Inventory what you have today. Passwords, reset flows, MFA/TOTP, SMS, SSO and API keys all interact with your new path. Write down every entry point before changing one.
  2. Add passkeys as an option for existing, signed-in users. A user who has already proved themselves registers a passkey from their account settings — no new identity questions, no risk of lockout.
  3. Put passkeys into the login form itself. Keep username and password fields, and let the browser surface passkeys through conditional UI (autofill shows the saved passkey alongside the password box).
  4. Handle the full sign-in state machine: no passkey yet, passkey available, cross-device sign-in by QR/hybrid handoff, user cancels, timeout, wrong device, and unsupported browser. Every branch needs a visible path back to the password form.
  5. Design recovery before you promote passwordless — and make recovery obvious: people lose devices, not memories. Recovery codes, a verified backup channel, and a documented support process must exist and be tested before any account can shed its password.
  6. Consider cross-device sync part of the product — it is the whole usability story. A passkey that only works on the phone you bought last year turns device loss into account loss.
  7. Instrument the flow. Track registration attempts, sign-in success and each failure reason (origin mismatch, unsupported, user canceled, verification failed). You need this data to know whether the fallback path is carrying the load.
  8. Roll out in stages. Opt-in first, then prompt after a successful sign-in, then make passkeys the default for accounts with proven recovery. Promote to passwordless last, and only for those accounts.
  9. Re-verify support on release day. Re-run the matrix above against current browser and OS versions — support is dated, and your launch is a date.

A practical example: storage and sign-in

An illustrative sketch of the server side — PHP for the application, with the browser API alongside. It shows the shape of the flow, not a complete implementation:

// 1. Issue a challenge bound to the session
$challenge = random_bytes(32);
$_SESSION['webauthn_challenge'] = base64_encode($challenge);

// 2. After the browser returns the assertion, verify before opening a session.
//    Use a maintained WebAuthn library: verification covers origin, rpId,
//    challenge, authenticator data and signature parsing (COSE/ASN.1).
$assertion = json_decode(file_get_contents('php://input'), true);

if (!webauthn_verify($assertion, base64_decode($_SESSION['webauthn_challenge']))) {
    http_response_code(401);
    exit;
}

// 3. Load the credential by id and open the session as any other login
$stmt = $pdo->prepare('SELECT user_id FROM passkeys WHERE credential_id = ?');
$stmt->execute([$assertion['id']]);
$row = $stmt->fetch();

And the browser half, with the password fields left in place so nothing becomes a dead end:

<form action="/login" method="post">
  <input type="email" name="email" autocomplete="username">
  <input type="password" name="password" autocomplete="current-password">
  <!-- browsers offer saved passkeys in this form -->
</form>

  • Store public material only: credential ID, public key, transports, and the timestamps you will want for review.
  • Validate the origin and relying party ID on the server even though the browser checks them — the browser is one party, not your whole threat model.
  • Server-side authorization is unchanged afterwards: a valid sign-in still has to check permissions on every request, the habit covered in never trust input in PHP.
  • The other half of the story is the device itself — the phone or laptop holding the keys, its ecosystem, and its security model. That is the territory of the 2026 developer hardware guide.

Common mistakes and honest limits

  • Replacing passwords on day one. One sync failure or unsupported browser becomes a support incident and a lockout story.
  • Registration from an unauthenticated state. It lets an attacker bind their own authenticator to a victim's account — the single most damaging implementation mistake here.
  • Reusing a challenge. Challenges must be fresh, unpredictable and single-use; that property is what makes the signature meaningful.
  • Ignoring cross-ecosystem users. A passkey created in one platform's keychain may need a QR/hybrid handoff to be used elsewhere; if your flow assumes same-device login, part of your users will bounce.
  • Recovery that is harder than the attack. If support can be socially engineered into a reset, the phishing resistance of the login is bypassed — align account recovery with the same identity standard.
  • Shared and kiosk accounts. Passkeys are per-person credentials; shared logins need a different design, not a shared device prompt.
  • Support is per platform and changes often. Anything fast-moving here — browser behavior, sync details, enterprise policy — deserves the same date stamp as the table above: as of October 2026, re-verify before shipping.
  • Passkeys are not encryption. They replace the authentication secret; end-to-end encryption is a separate design decision about keys, data and who can read it.

Key takeaways

  • Passkeys remove the secret instead of adding another code: a device-held private key, a server-held public key, and a signature over a one-time challenge.
  • Phishing resistance is structural — origin binding is enforced by the browser and authenticator, not by user vigilance.
  • Support exists broadly on current browsers and operating systems, but the table above is a dated snapshot: verify before shipping.
  • Roll out beside passwords first, instrument every failure reason, prove recovery, then promote accounts to passwordless.
  • Registration must be authenticated, challenges must be single-use, and recovery is part of the security model.
  • End-to-end encryption, passkeys and account recovery are three separate decisions — do not let one stand in for the others.

The practical next step is small: open your login form, add the passkey registration entry point for already-signed-in users, and wire up conditional UI beside your existing password fields. Ship that, watch the failure reasons, and let the data tell you when an account has earned the right to lose its password.
